# Visual design and anti-generic playbook

## Read the room

Infer the audience, category, emotional goal, trust requirements, content density, and existing brand. State a one-line design read before coding. Choose an intentional design system or aesthetic; do not mix visual languages section by section.

Set three dials from 1-10:

- **Layout variance:** 1 is conventional and symmetric; 10 is editorial, asymmetric, and art-directed.
- **Motion intensity:** 1 is nearly static; 10 is a motion-led experience.
- **Visual density:** 1 is sparse; 10 is information-rich.

Use the brief to choose values. Do not default every marketing page to 7/6/4.

## Establish the system

Define tokens for:

- display and body typography, weights, line height, tracking, and readable measure;
- semantic foreground/background/accent/status colors with contrast;
- spacing rhythm and page gutters;
- container widths and grid logic;
- one coherent radius family;
- border, shadow, blur, and material rules;
- layer/z-index scale;
- motion durations, easings, distances, and stagger.

Preserve an effective existing system. In a redesign, classify elements as preserve, refine, replace, or remove before changing them.

## Compose pages with rhythm

- Make the primary promise and action clear in the first viewport.
- Limit the hero to the elements that establish meaning and action. Avoid stuffing trust strips, badges, metadata, and secondary explanations into it.
- Use typography, whitespace, alignment, and imagery before reaching for cards.
- Vary section families across a long page: editorial text, full-bleed media, asymmetric composition, proof, process, interactive demonstration, comparison, and focused CTA.
- Avoid three or more repeated image/text zigzags and repeated split headers.
- Use real evidence and imagery. Do not fabricate dashboards, client logos, reviews, awards, or metrics.
- Make empty space intentional; do not fill every gap with decoration.

## Remove common AI design tells

Reject by default:

- generic purple-blue gradients and glowing blobs unrelated to the brand;
- Inter/system font as an unconsidered default;
- three equal cards for every section;
- nested cards, excessive pills, rounded icon tiles, and borders on every row;
- vague uppercase eyebrows above every heading;
- decorative charts, progress bars, code windows, or maps with fake data;
- "Trusted by" text with invented or plain-text logos;
- repeated bento grids with identical white cells;
- arbitrary glassmorphism, oversized radii, or gradients used as a substitute for art direction;
- every element fading upward on scroll;
- placeholder people, "Acme," "Jane Doe," fake version labels, and ornamental location/time/weather strips.

One unusual motif is stronger than many disconnected effects. Tie it to the business: material, process, geography, craft, product behavior, or customer transformation.

## Responsive and accessible composition

- Design mobile composition, not merely stacked desktop.
- Use fluid type and spacing with tested bounds.
- Keep navigation operable on one desktop line and usable at narrow widths.
- Maintain 44px touch targets, visible focus, semantic controls, labels, and logical order.
- Test 200% zoom, long names, localization expansion, missing images, and content extremes.
- Prevent horizontal overflow and sticky/floating collisions at breakpoint boundaries.
- Keep contrast at WCAG AA or better and never rely on color alone.

## Final visual acceptance

Verify:

1. The site is recognizable without its logo.
2. Hierarchy is clear before decoration.
3. Layout families vary without losing coherence.
4. Typography and imagery fit the actual business.
5. Every visual claim is real.
6. Mobile feels designed, not collapsed.
7. The signature motif is useful or meaningful.
8. Removing any effect that does not improve comprehension, action, trust, or emotion makes the page better.
